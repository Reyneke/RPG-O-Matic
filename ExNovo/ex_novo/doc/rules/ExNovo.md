## Introduction

You (and up to three friends) take on the role of chroniclers, sages, and historians recording the founding and development of a settlement. Your goal is to witness your city's growth and remember its stories.

During each game you will pick inspiring prompts, interpret them, and draw your city's features onto a collaborative map. As you play, your city's history will unfold, and this map will change. Tokens will be moved around and factions will rise and fall. By the time you're done, your map will represent a fictional place with a real sense of history.

- **Players:** 1 to 4 (solo or cooperatively)
- **Playtime:** 1 to 3 hours
- **Requires:** Paper, pens and 3 six-sided dice

### Phases of Play

The game is divided into four phases:

1. **Setup Phase** — You determine the age, size, and importance of the city you will build during play. You'll set aside the appropriate number of tokens and make your first marks on the map.
2. **Founding Phase** — You establish the basics, such as the city's purpose or the terrain of the surrounding area. You take turns rolling for different prompts and interpreting the results. Each prompt comes with instructions for the active player to add features to the map and move tokens around. This provides an interesting starting situation.
3. **Development Phase** — You play out the exciting history of your city and watch its map evolve. Taking turns, you randomly determine a historic event and describe it according to the story of your city. Events range from brutal wars and technological discoveries to new fashions, and will add new factions, destroy districts, unearth resources, and much more. This phase also deals with any unspent tokens.
4. **Topping Out Phase** — A few quick steps tie up any loose ends as you put your finishing touches down.

> **Note about the Development Phase:** The age of your city has an impact on the duration of the game: it defines the number of turns you'll play during the Development Phase.

### Sequence Overview

- **It takes ten steps to found your city.** First you draw the geography and terrain of the area onto your map. Then you add your first resource and first district. Lastly, you establish your two starting factions and name the city.
- **Each turn** it's up to you to determine what the picked event means in the context of your city. Building on each other, the events will form your city's history. Usually each turn will have you add or change a feature on the map as the city evolves.

## Preparation

To play Ex Novo you will need:

- **Three six-sided dice** — abbreviated as D6, ideally in different colors.
- **Up to 25 citizen tokens** — like glass beads, meeples, or houses made out of wood.
- **Up to 25 power tokens** — like poker chips, coins, or little wooden blocks.
- **A large sheet of paper** — that can be comfortably seen and reached by everyone.
- **A few index cards** — to note down factions and record historic events.
- **Pens or pencils** — writing tools for everyone, possibly in different colors.

## City Elements

The different elements of a city are:

| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| **Citizen tokens** | Represent people |
| **Power tokens** | A measure of potential |
| **Factions** | Organized groups with goals |
| **The timeline** | To keep track of events |
| **Regions** | Abstract units of space |
| **Terrain** | Notable geography |
| **Landmarks** | Places of interest |
| **Resources** | Things of value to the city |
| **Districts** | Areas where people live |

- **Regions** are abstract units of space. They are created by criss-crossing your paper with lines. The space between these lines forms the different regions of your map.
- **Terrain** refers to areas of notable geography: mountains, rivers, forests, and more. These are created during the Founding Phase and are drawn onto the map.
- **Resources** are sources of value to the city. These can be food sources, natural materials, strategic positions, and more. They are represented as symbols on your map.
- **Landmarks** are places of interest, usually important structures in and around your city: bridges, temples, and more. They are added to your map as simple symbols.
- **Districts** are where people live. These are drawn on your map as shapes and can vary in density, as symbolized by the number of citizen tokens placed on them.
- **Citizen tokens** represent the population of your city. These are placed on districts as a measure of density.
- **Power tokens** provide a measure of influence. They are placed on factions to display their relative power, or on features of your map to show their untapped potential.
- **Factions** are groups of people with shared goals. Each faction is kept on its own index card. They are the key drivers of a city's story and drama. As you play, factions will appear, rise and fall and even vanish entirely, their shifting influence represented by power tokens.

## Sequence of Play

Below you will find a description of the different phases and steps that make up the flow of the game. During many steps you will look up results in the tables at the back of the book. The entry you pick there will provide you with a prompt to interpret and some actions to shape your city's features. You can learn more about both in the [Features, Tokens & Actions](#features-tokens--actions) chapter.

### Aligning Assumptions

Talking about your city's intended setting and the session's goals before starting play is very useful. All players should agree on a setting they are excited about and familiar with. If half of the table is going for serious and useful while the other half is aiming for wacky and silly, then confusion or disappointment are likely.

---

### 1. Setup Phase

The game's first phase is the Setup Phase, where you determine the scale and scope of your city. These choices meaningfully impact both how the game plays out as well as its final result. All steps in this phase are collaborative and executed together.

#### 1.1. Target Age

Your first step is to determine how old the city will be when the game ends. Pick an entry from the [Scale–Age table](#scale--age) (page 34) as a group. As with all table entries, it will be your responsibility to interpret the prompt accordingly and establish truths in the fiction of your city.

The entry will tell you to draw a given number of boxes on an index card or piece of paper to create your timeline. Each box represents one historic event that you will play through in order to create your city. Target age determines how long the game will last.

#### 1.2. Target Size

Next you have to determine how big your settlement will be at the end of play. Decide together and pick an entry from the [Scale–Size table](#scale--size) (page 34).

Interpret the description together and then draw a given number of lines to split the map into regions. The bigger the city, the more lines you have to draw. You can do this in turns or all at the same time. You then set aside a number of citizen tokens. These represent the future citizens of your city. The more citizen tokens a city has access to, the bigger it will become.

> **Note:** We recommend you pick a Target Size at least equal to the Target Age, thus giving the city time to grow to that size.

#### 1.3. Target Importance

Finally, you have to decide how influential your city will be in the grand scheme of things. Collaboratively pick an entry from the [Scale–Influence table](#scale--influence) (page 35). If you pick a high degree of influence, note that the city will not start out at this level of importance, but will reach the intended level of notoriety after you've played out its history.

This step asks that you set aside a given number of power tokens. Power tokens represent the future influence of the city's factions. The more important the city, the more tokens you set aside. Together with the unused citizen tokens, these power tokens form the **growth pool** – the city's potential.

> **Open Ended:** If you prefer to not predetermine size, influence, and/or age, but rather discover one or more of these during play, simply pick the largest option in that category. For example, if you want to keep time indeterminate, pick *Ancient* and mark 25 boxes on the timeline. Then simply stop playing once you feel you've reached a point in the fiction that works for you. Any unused tokens related to the open-ended target left at the end of the Development Phase are simply discarded.

> **Uncertain Scale:** Since the three steps of this phase all have a big impact on how the game plays, we recommend picking them based on your goals. That said, it's no accident that these tables all have 6 options. If you want to, you can roll for them and let the dice surprise you instead of picking the entries.

---

### 2. Founding Phase

In this phase you determine the starting conditions of your settlement together. Each step has you consult a different table, which will lead you through the city's founding. Contrary to the previous phase where the steps were collaborative, in this phase each player performs a different step (except for the last one, which is a joint effort).

When playing with multiple players, first determine a starting player. Our suggestion is to begin with the player who last discovered something new about the city they live in. They perform the first step of this phase. Once they are done, the player to their left becomes the active player and performs the next step, even if a step is repeated.

#### 2.1. Major Geography

To find out what kind of landscape was chosen for the founding of this city, you roll on the [Terrain–Geography table](#terrain--geography) (page 36).

Interpret the prompt, draw the major terrain shape onto the map and name it. For example, for a peninsula you could add water on three sides of your map.

#### 2.2. Terrain Features (x4)

Time to discover the topography of this area as you roll on the [Terrain–Features table](#terrain--features) (page 37) to find a prompt for a new terrain feature.

Draw this terrain feature onto your map and then name it. **Repeat this step four times.**

#### 2.3. Founding Location

Let's find out what about this place was special enough to settle here. Roll on the [Purpose–Location table](#purpose--location) (page 38) to find out more.

As before, interpret your prompt and then draw a resource onto the map. Next, name the resource and move two power tokens from the growth pool onto the resource. Finally, if you feel it is fitting, you can add a landmark for the resource.

#### 2.4. Settlement Decision

This step tells us who initiated the founding of the settlement. Roll on the [Purpose–Decision table](#purpose--decision) (page 39). The options range from an accident all the way to a divine command.

During this step you draw the city's first district, roughly filling out a region. Then you move a citizen token from the growth pool to the district. Lastly, make sure to name your district.

> **Creative Freedom:** While you are the active player, you have free reign to interpret the prompt and perform the associated actions in any way you want. However, your fellow players are there to help, so feel free to ask them for ideas, input, or to chip in with the drawing!

#### 2.5. Leading Power

The people have settled, and now it's time to figure out the sources of power in this city, and who holds most of it. Roll on the [Power–Paradigm table](#power--paradigm) (page 40) and interpret the table entry.

Then create your first and most powerful faction: note the name and symbol of this faction on an index card. You then move 2 power tokens from the growth pool onto the faction. Lastly, if you want, you may add a landmark for that faction.

#### 2.6. Secondary Power

To hold unquestioned power is a rarity, so it's prudent to ask who else has some pull within your community and how they relate to the ones in charge.

Roll on the [Power–Relationship table](#power--relationship) (page 41) and interpret another table entry to create your city's second faction.

Write the name and symbol of the faction on an index card. Then move 1 power token from the growth pool onto that faction. If you want, you can also add a landmark for this group.

> **More Factions:** Step 2.6 can be repeated to add more starting factions to your city. Maybe you want a more complex web of power to start with, or maybe each player wants to contribute a faction. We believe that the default of two starting factions provides a good balance of stability and drama. Three factions can also work, but we do think four is the upper limit – after that things just get too complicated.

#### 2.7. Name the City

The final step before you can dive into the city's unfolding history is to name it. Each player can suggest one name. If you're hard pressed for one, simply pick a feature of the terrain or the surroundings and use that as the basis for a name. Starting with something like *Gulltown* or *Lower Darkwood* is perfectly fine.

When everyone has offered a name (or declined to), either decide which name to pick collaboratively, or, if no quick consensus is reached, pick at random. Then write the city's name at the edge of your map. The player whose name got picked becomes the active player who begins the next phase of the game.

---

### 3. Development Phase

During this phase you uncover the unpredictable events that change and shape your city as time passes. During the Founding Phase you rotated players after each step, but in this phase the active player performs all the steps of the phase. This is considered their turn. Once their turn is done, the player to their left becomes the active player and begins their turn.

Each turn you check off one box in the timeline and you repeat this sequence until all boxes are filled.

#### 3.1. Historic Event

You roll 3D6 to determine a random event from the [Events table](#events) (page 42). You then interpret its prompt, building on the previously established story of the settlement. The text within each prompt is meant as inspiration. If your creativity takes you somewhere else, feel free to follow it! You may also ask your fellow players for inspiration. Having everyone's contributions build upon each other can be great fun, but remember that the active player has final say.

Almost all events will ask you to perform the actions listed. Many of these actions have you add a feature to the map, but in some cases you may be asked to change or remove a feature, or to move some tokens. Actions in parentheses (noted like this) are entirely optional.

If an action is impossible (maybe because you are tasked to remove a feature that doesn't exist in your city) you can ignore it instead.

#### 3.2. Natural Growth (optional)

After you have resolved your event, you may move up to two tokens from the growth pool to the city, as long as there are any remaining. The tokens moved can be any combination of power and citizen tokens. This step represents the natural development of the city and can stand in for a bump in births and immigration, or a consolidation of power and influence.

- **Citizen tokens** can be used to either add a new district or to increase the density of an existing one.
- **Power tokens** can be put onto a faction or a feature of the city.

No matter what kind of tokens you pick, you can move them to the same or to different targets, if you want.

#### 3.3. Record History

The final step in your turn is to mark off a box on the timeline to keep track of the elapsed turns. We also recommend that you write down the name of the event next to the checkbox to keep a record of your city's history. If you are so inclined, you can also write down a sentence or two about the event itself while the next player starts their turn.

---

### 4. Topping Out Phase

When the basic shell of a building is done, you celebrate its completion by "topping out". So once all your historic events are resolved, it's time to wrap up your settlement. During this phase you move all remaining citizen and power tokens out of the growth pool, and put your final touches on the map.

Each step of this phase is executed by a different player and should a step need to be repeated, the active player will change as usual. The final step is a collaborative one. This is just like the Founding Phase again.

#### 4.1. Remaining Growth (until no tokens)

This step is essentially a repetition of step 3.2, except it is no longer optional. You need to move two tokens from the growth pool to the city, if possible. As before, use each citizen token to add a new district or to increase the density of an existing one. Power tokens can either be moved onto a faction or a city feature.

Note that, like in the Natural Growth step, you can take two tokens of the same type and move them to the same or to different targets, if you like.

**Repeat this step until the growth pool is empty.**

#### 4.2. Rename the City

As a final step you give the city a second name. This can be an official renaming, a name in its citizens' native tongue, a variant through linguistic drift, a positive or disparaging nickname, an official subtitle, or a common epithet.

Just as with the initial naming (step 2.7), each player can suggest one name. As before, you make the decision collaboratively. If that fails or just takes too long, pick randomly. Then add the city's second name to the edge of your map.

To finish the game, the player whose city name got picked adds a new, unique landmark to the city, naming it after themselves. Feel free to just use a part of your name. If your name is Peter it could be *Peter's Tower*, the *Pergate*, *Ter Market*…

## Features, Tokens & Actions

Your city consists of different elements that are added and changed over time. Among these, **features** are the parts of the city drawn onto the map, while **factions** and the **timeline** are kept on separate index cards. **Tokens** are small playing pieces that move around during play.

The various actions you perform will ask you to add, change, or even remove your city's features and factions, while your tokens will move between the growth pool and the various features and factions of the city.

### Adding Features

When an action instructs you to add a feature to your city, that usually means you draw a representation of it onto the map. Not only that, you also **name** the feature and write its name in small letters next to its drawing. You can find more details on how to add the different kinds of features in this chapter.

> **Example:** During the Founding Phase, a player is instructed to add a forest to the map. They pick a section of the map, draw an organic shape around it with a green marker, and fill it with a few trees. They decide to call it "Mistwood", because it's shrouded in dense, chill mists for much longer than surrounding areas. They write its name along the forest's border on the map.

### Transforming Features

Some actions have you transform an existing feature. This indicates that its meaning in the fiction and story of your city changes. A museum is expanded, a bridge falls to disrepair, a district is gentrified, or a forest is destroyed by a plague of bugs. Whatever this change is, it should be substantial and, if it relates to a feature on the map, it should find its expression there.

This transformation can also include the feature's name. If applicable, feel free to rename it. This could be a completely new official name, a shortening, a nickname, or some manner of linguistic drift. Maybe all you do is replace or shift some letters around to make the name faster or easier to pronounce.

> **Example:** During the Development Phase, the "Ecological imbalance" event asks the active player to transform a terrain feature. Given the story so far, they decide to transform the "Mistwood". A change in the local fauna leads to the area suddenly becoming much drier, losing its misty quality. They decide to rename it to "Kindlemist".

### Removing Features

When you are asked to remove a feature it doesn't mean you simply cross out or erase the corresponding symbol from the map. Cities are physical things and buildings don't just vanish. Building materials are reused, old ruins persist, or temples are repurposed as marketplaces. People will often find ways to make use of what is around them and will remember what was important to them.

When you remove a feature, change its representation on the map. If it makes sense you may leave a new landmark behind:
- A district is deserted and no longer in use? Draw a few derelict houses in its area.
- A temple is abandoned? Replace it with a landmark of its ruin.

Additionally, **withdraw all tokens** from the feature you're removing. By default, they move back to the growth pool, but you can opt to place all or just some of them somewhere else if it makes sense within the story of your city.

> **Example:** Later during development, the "Wildfires" event ravages the countryside. The acting player knows what to do: Kindlemist catches fire and burns down. They erase the trees and put in some burnt down husks instead. They also decide to rename the area to "The Ash Fields".

### Naming Features

Names are powerful, even if finding a fitting one on the fly can sometimes be tricky. Try not to let this slow you down. Remember that names often change, so don't aim for perfection. And if you're struggling, just go with something very obvious and simple. It's perfectly fine if a forest is just called *Greenwood* or a river gets the name *Fast Water*.

Alternatively, pick a name based on something you can see (*Shingle Hills*), something that's in your pockets (*Key Tower*), or use a part of your own name.

## Tokens

Ex Novo makes use of two different kinds of tokens: **citizen tokens** and **power tokens**. These tokens can be assigned to a feature of the city or a faction, which means they are placed on the feature's representation on the map or the faction's note card.

> **Important:** The different token types cannot be used interchangeably: a power token cannot be used in place of a citizen token or vice versa.

### Growth Pool

All tokens start in the **growth pool**, representing the potential of your city, and any unused tokens return to it. We recommend using a small container next to your map.

At the end of the game, any remaining tokens (citizen and power) in the growth pool will be moved into the city.

### Citizen Tokens

Citizen tokens stand in for the people living in the city. As you play, more and more citizen tokens will move from the growth pool into the districts of the city.

There may be moments when citizen tokens are removed from the map as people die or leave the city. In these cases move the tokens back into the pool.

### Power Tokens

Power tokens represent the might of the city. As your city's factions grow in power, tokens will be moved from the growth pool into the city.

This means that power tokens are placed onto a feature on the map – generally a resource, but they can also be put on landmarks, terrain, or even districts. In this way the power tokens represent potential, unclaimed power or the importance of that element of the city.

Power tokens can also be placed on factions, representing their actualized influence. As the factions gain power they generally do so by taking the unclaimed power tokens from the city, essentially controlling that part of the city.

As events unfold, some factions will gain power while others will lose it. This is represented by the power tokens moving between the factions, back to the city, or even back into the growth pool, as resources stop being exploited and alliances shift.

### Moving Tokens

Sometimes an action asks you to move a specific kind of token. If not otherwise specified, you can decide where you pick the token from (the growth pool, a faction, the city) and where you move it to.

However you can only move a token to a place it can legally be put on:
- A **citizen token** can only be in the growth pool or on a district.
- A **power token** can only be in the growth pool, on a feature of the city, or on a faction.

When an action requires a token to be moved, it is only possible if a token is available. For example, when founding a new district one citizen token is required. Without it, the city is at capacity and no new district may be founded. The actions on the following pages will specify the sources from which a token can be taken.

Remember that as part of removing a feature or faction, its tokens need to be moved elsewhere.

> **Example:** During the Development Phase, an event instructs the active player to remove a landmark. They pick the old harbor, which currently holds a power token. This token represents the influence of the harbor masters.
>
> With the harbor defunct, the token could be moved back to the growth pool, but the active player chooses to put it onto a nearby district instead. The influence of the harbor masters isn't gone, but, without the structure of the harbor, it has moved to the fisherfolk nearby.

## Factions

Factions are crucial both to keeping the history of the city alive and in moving it forward. Factions can be very specific groups of people like the council of elders, or they can be a more nebulous movement like rebellious factory workers. Factions can also represent forces outside the city such as the tyrant king or the marauding merfolk pirates.

It is important that the factions you add have some power to affect the city, either from the top down, the bottom up, or even from the outside. Because of this, factions are closely linked to power tokens. These represent their influence over the people of the city and beyond.

### Adding a Faction

When you add a faction, take an index card, write down its name and add a simple symbol representing it.

To form a new faction you need to be able to put one power token onto its card. Unless specified otherwise, you take this token from one of the three following sources, **in order**:

1. **From a feature on the map** — This represents a faction basing its power on that element of the city.
2. **From another faction** — This represents a faction splintering off or coming to power in conflict with that faction.
3. **From the growth pool** — This represents a faction of the city empowered by an outside source.

Only if there are no tokens of the first type present, can you pick from the second one and so on.

Adding a faction also allows you to add a landmark to represent it on your map, if you want. This could be the headquarters, a frequent, secret meeting place or something else of value to the faction and its supporters. Note that the destruction of this place does not destroy the faction.

### Removing a Faction

If a faction is removed, all its power tokens are redistributed among the city, its factions, and/or the growth pool as is appropriate for your story:
- Tokens moving to other factions represent the other forces filling out the power vacuum.
- Power tokens moving to features of the city represent the now unused potential of these elements.
- Any power tokens moving to the growth pool symbolize the city losing influence.

If the faction had a headquarter landmark (or any other landmarks associated with it), then the faction's destruction will usually also transform that landmark. Generally, these places will get repurposed, not destroyed.

If the last remaining faction is removed, add a new faction that succeeds it, and, if possible, give this new faction a power token from the destroyed faction. Power, after all, doesn't stay unused for long.

### Gaining Power

When a faction gains power, take one or more power tokens (how many tokens you move is your choice, unless specified) and move them to the faction gaining power.

Unless specified otherwise, the possible source is the same as for adding a faction:
1. Try to first take unclaimed power tokens from one or more features of the map.
2. If there are not enough unclaimed tokens, take tokens from other factions within the city.
3. If that's not an option, you can take tokens from the growth pool.

Some actions may specify a source for these power tokens, such as "a faction gains power from another faction", which means you should try to take tokens from another faction first. If there is no suitable source, use the growth pool instead. If the growth pool is empty the action is impossible and is skipped.

### Losing Power

If a faction loses power, move one or more (your choice, unless specified) of its power tokens back to the growth pool. If a faction without power tokens would lose power, **remove the faction instead**.

There are cases when the action that instructs you to lose power also specifies a target for the power tokens: "A faction loses power to a district", for example. This means that the respective power tokens are to be moved onto a district of your choice. If this specifies a target that is not applicable to your city, return the tokens to the growth pool instead.

There are also actions that instruct you to lose a specific amount of power tokens unless you'd prefer something else to happen: "Lose 1 power or remove a district", for example. In this case it's your choice which of the two available actions to perform. To select an action to lose power, however, you need to be able to return that many power tokens to the growth pool, or remove a faction (which only counts as losing 1 power).

> **Example:** During the Development Phase, an event asks for a faction to gain power. The active player decides that the event aligns strongly with the Watchmen faction, and that they should gain 2 power. However, there is only 1 unclaimed power token. The active player moves it to the Watchmen faction and takes a second token from another faction.

## Timeline

The timeline is your historic record of the city's development. During the Founding Phase you create it by drawing one box for each historic event you will discover. It's recommended to put the timeline on a separate index card or piece of paper, and not on the map directly.

Then, as you play, you check off the boxes to keep track of your progress. You can use this opportunity to write down the name of the current event and so provide a brief chronicle of sorts. If you feel so inclined, you might want to add a sentence or two describing the event in more detail.

You can also move the timeline between players during the game to indicate the current active player.

---

## Regions

A region is an abstract unit of space that provides a sense of scale and helps you shape your city. Regions can have individual characteristics, such as a difference in vegetation or elevation, but that is not required. Be aware that regions don't need to be regular in shape or even similarly sized. A variety of different regions is good.

### Drawing Regions

When you establish the size of your city, you are asked to split the map into regions using lines. When doing so you are neither limited to straight lines, nor to lines that go from one edge of the paper to the other. You can use circles or squiggles to create an organic structure or draw a line that starts and ends in a random spot on the paper. Regions are the areas between these lines.

We recommend drawing these region lines with a lighter shade than everything else, since you will draw all other features of your city on top of them. This way, you're less likely to confuse a region's lines with a river or a district boundary.

> **Drawing the Map:** When you're drawing during play it's not about creating an accurate representation or a perfect picture. Your map is a working document, so speed and readability are much more important than pretty visuals. Even more so when a mudslide could destroy that painstakingly drawn house at any time. Keep things fast and loose, but make sure everyone understands what you're drawing.

## Terrain

Terrain is the catch-all term for all things topographical and geographical. Anything from mountain ranges and lakes, to volcanoes and rivers is a piece of terrain. When you start filling your map with terrain, we recommend leaving about a third to a half of your map uncluttered enough for your city to have space to grow into.

### Adding Terrain

When you add terrain to the map, simply draw a shape that best represents the feature. Simple elements like rivers or singular hills can be drawn directly. For larger areas like a massive forest, drawing every single tree will slow play down too much.

Instead, draw an outline for the general terrain feature, and then add a few simple symbols of the landscape represented within it. A set of three waves standing in for a giant lake or a scattering of a few trees for a massive forest should be enough. Drawing an outline in a fitting color can also be useful. And, if you really want to draw the entire forest, you can do so during another player's turn.

### Removing Terrain

If you are asked to remove a terrain feature, transform it accordingly. Usually large scale changes like this leave something behind: a river dries out to leave a dead riverbed or a cliff collapses to rubble.

Sometimes change like this can be represented by transforming the symbols inside the outline of the terrain feature: the trees of a forest, for instance, get replaced by burnt out husks after a forest fire. Other times you may simply want to erase and redraw the feature.

## Landmarks

Landmarks are buildings or places that stand out. These may be elements like an important statue, a major trade route, a fishing harbor, or a wizard's tower. Landmarks are the kind of feature most citizens would be familiar with.

> **Note:** Landmarks have no size or shape restrictions. A landmark could be the massive stone walls encircling the city, the imperial road crossing the entire map, or a tiny restaurant of great renown.

### Adding Landmarks

When you add a landmark to the map, you get to decide what it is, and then draw a simple representation of it on the map (wherever it seems appropriate). Maybe a new statue is erected in honor of a past ruler, a forgotten temple is discovered in the woods, or a development has made the tiny harbor so much more important.

### Removing Landmarks

If you're instructed to remove a landmark, this means that a landmark of your choice falls into disuse, is destroyed, or otherwise degraded: a tower is demolished, a bridge collapses. Transform the symbol to one representing the landmark's new state. If its physical representation is completely destroyed, you can remove it altogether.

Remember that any tokens placed on the landmark need to be moved elsewhere.

## Resources

Resources are where wealth and power for the city and its factions come from. They can be concrete goods, such as gold or abundant fish, but they can also be representations of more abstract things like rare skills or specialized knowledge.

Resources are closely linked to power tokens, being one of the main ways for those tokens to move from the growth pool and into the city, where the different factions can then claim them.

### Adding Resources

When you add a resource, it signifies that a new source of value has been discovered or created. A resource is always depicted on the map with a symbol of your choice.

To represent the potential and power that a new resource offers to the city, put one or more power tokens from the growth pool onto the resource. If there are no power tokens left in the pool, you should instead take any number of power tokens from factions or the other features of the city. This represents the fact that the appearance of this new resource has shifted the balance of power.

If it makes sense, you may also add a landmark alongside the resource. For example, the landmark for "abundant fishing opportunities" could be the fisher's harbor.

### Removing Resources

A resource is generally removed when it has been destroyed, lost, or otherwise no longer provides appropriate value. Transform the symbol of the resource and the landmark associated with it appropriately, and don't forget to move any power tokens still on it somewhere else. In most cases these should go back into the growth pool, but if it makes narrative sense, you can shift them to a faction or a feature of the city.

## Districts

Districts are where the people of the city live, work, and socialize. In Ex Novo they are generally considered mixed use areas, but you can designate them for residential, industrial, mixed, or other use if you feel it is needed.

### District Density

Districts are closely linked to citizen tokens and have a density from 0 to 3, represented by the number of tokens placed within the district:

| Density | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **0** | An abandoned district (rare) |
| **1** | A sparse and spacious district — a rural area or a hill peppered with expensive mansions |
| **2** | Urban living: mostly streets and houses with a few plazas |
| **3** | Buildings piling on top of each other |

### Adding Districts

When an action instructs you to add a district, you create a new one by taking a citizen token from the growth pool and putting it on an appropriate space on the map. If the growth pool is empty, you can choose to take a citizen token from another district or you can skip this action entirely.

Then draw an irregular shape around the citizen token to demarcate the district's boundaries. It should roughly fit within one region. If your regions are vastly different in size, just make sure districts have sizes that are comparable to one another.

For a more contemporary feel, use angled lines for district boundaries instead of organic shapes. We recommend not drawing individual houses and instead leaving room for interesting landmarks to appear within the districts.

### Removing Districts

When you are asked to remove a district, it means that it has become unlivable. Distribute all remaining citizen tokens from that district among the other districts of your city and/or the growth pool. Tokens moving to other districts represent shifting populations within the city, whereas tokens moving back into the growth pool represent citizens either leaving the city or dying.

If there are any power tokens present on the district, return them to the growth pool, or move them to factions and/or features of your city. Then change the district's representation on the map to reflect its new state. Since it's impossible for that many houses to vanish without a trace, don't simply erase the district's outline. Instead add a symbol of its remains or ruins left in its stead.

### Increasing Density

When an action asks you to increase the density of a district, you need to take a citizen token from the growth pool and put it on an already existing district. If there are no more tokens available in the growth pool, you can instead decide to take a token from another district – or you can ignore this action.

If you want or need to increase the density of a district that's already at its maximum of three tokens, the people's need for housing will persist. Instead of building more living space in the already crammed district, a new district of improvised and cheap housing is built. Place the citizen token wherever there's space and material to build a new district nearby, and do so. This is called **district spillover**.

### Reducing Density

When you have to reduce the density of a district, move one or more tokens from that district back to the growth pool. If a district reaches density 0, it's effectively deserted. If you are asked to reduce the density of a district that is already at 0, **remove that district instead**.

> **Example:** During the Development Phase, the active player uses their natural growth step to move two citizen tokens to a district of density 2. This puts it above the maximum density of 3, and so one of the tokens is moved to a nearby region instead, creating a new district through the district spillover rule.

### Zooming In

While building your city you've mostly been maintaining a bird's eye view. If you want to zoom in closer, the following prompts might inspire you to look at your creation through the lens of time.

#### Past

Examine the crucial moments of your city's history and flesh them out with details and heft. Look at specific events on your timeline and ask yourself:

- Has this event been immortalized in art and architecture? How?
- Did the local cuisine evolve in response to the event? How?
- Did this event give birth to any district- or city-wide rituals? Why?
- Did it impact how people and things move within the city? How?
- Has the way people relax and wind down been affected? How?
- Is this event being retold in stories or historic accounts? By whom?

#### Present

If you want to explore what everyday life feels like for average people on your city's streets, think about your different districts through the following lenses:

- What signs, colors and images are visible on the street?
- What is the average building like? What is it built out of?
- Where do people congregate and meet? Why?
- How do people entertain themselves?
- Is the city's history visible? Where?
- How are nearby landmarks integrated into daily life? By whom?

#### Future

Cities are perpetual works in progress. Find out more about their future by looking at your various factions through one or more of the following lenses:

- Who rules the faction, and who's next in line for leadership?
- What danger is the faction facing? Are there preparations put in place?
- What opportunities is the faction looking to exploit?
- What new allies is it trying to court?
- How well does the faction adapt to change? Is it flexible or rigid?
- How do they feel about their position in the balance of power?

> **Do It Yourself:** Ex Novo is a collaborative and creative tool at its heart. We structured the rules in a deliberate way to help you generate memorable results, but the game is quite robust – if you want to change something, feel free to do so! We made this game for you to play with, and that includes the rules.

### Diverse Development

If you are looking to spice up the development of your city, or to try and play in different ways, here are some alternative rules and ideas you can experiment with.

#### Online Play

Use an online white-board. Decide how to represent tokens – preferably via simple shapes that can be moved around. If the app of your choice supports post-it notes, use these for keeping track of your factions and timeline.

#### Cities for Kids

If you want to play with young players, we recommend a simpler version of the rules: whenever you roll on a table, you just care about the name of the prompt, ignoring any actions or descriptions. Then all you do is draw it onto the map. No factions, no tokens, no extra rules. Simply roll, look at the event's name, and draw.

#### Less Random

This option gives you a bit more control over the results you roll during play. At the start of the game, every player gets five six-sided dice of a special color. These are called **correction dice**.

During play, whenever a player gets a dice result they are not happy with, they may roll one of their correction dice and have it replace one of the dice they rolled normally. If that's not good enough, players can keep rolling their remaining correction dice until they either exhaust them or get an outcome they like.

#### Wild Foundation

The tables of the Foundation Phase are all rolled with 2D6, meaning that you are more likely to roll results towards the middle of the table, making 7 the most likely result. This is on purpose, as the more common outcomes have been assigned to numbers on the tables with a higher likelihood.

If you prefer a more volatile foundation you can roll a twelve-sided dice instead. If a 1 comes up, roll two D12s and interpret the two results from the table together (reroll any further 1s on your dice).

#### Temporal Determinism

If you want to base your city development on a specific timeline, you can simply note down the number of events you have during the Setup Phase instead of picking your city's age.

Then, pick specific events that you know will happen at the appropriate points in time and write them next to the empty boxes on your timeline. Play through the Development Phase as normal, and when you hit a predefined event, do not roll for it, but play out the one you have already selected instead.

#### Population Groups

If you want a more detailed look at the citizenry of your city, you could use different colored citizen tokens to represent the different population groups within your city.

### A Permanent Map

Preserving your city requires a map that you will still be able to understand some time from now.

#### Style and Color

If you don't have a lot of experience crafting maps, it pays to look at other maps you like for inspiration, and take it from there.

Whenever you add a color to your map, do so with intention. A color should always communicate something relevant. Drawing all bodies of water in blue makes sense, whereas making your landmarks blue as well might be confusing.

#### Text on the Map

Typography is an important aspect of cartography. Following a simple hierarchy can make maps legible and pleasing to look at. We recommend using three font size categories:

- The **largest** one for the city's name.
- The **medium sized** font for names of areas, districts and terrain features.
- The **smallest** one for naming points of interest, like landmarks and resources.

As to how text is placed on the map, there are several options: you can put names in boxes, have them follow the shapes of linear elements, or place them above or next to the relevant elements. Whatever you pick, stick with it – consistency is important.

#### Non-Physical Information

Before you create the final map, you should pause and consider what non-physical information you want to add to the map, and how you want to convey it.

- For your factions, you could add a box or shield-shape with the iconography and name of each faction – possibly at the edge of your map. Alternatively, you could add faction symbols next to headquarter landmarks. In this case you might want to use a legend to add the faction's name without cluttering up the map.
- If you decide to represent the various power tokens of your city, you could add one or more stars next to the feature or faction. Another option would be to draw the faction or feature impressively enough to convey its importance.
- If you want to feature the settlement's timeline, we suggest you mark off a box at the edge of your map before you start drawing your city, and then write down (at least) the names of the events in chronological order.

#### Symbols and Buildings

From landmarks and faction icons, to rivers and terrain features, it is through symbols that the different elements of a map are depicted. Try to keep your symbols legible, use clean lines and simple shapes, and, if possible, attempt to use similar drawing styles for similar elements.

#### Depicting District Density

A straightforward way to depict density is by drawing detailed streets and houses to fill a district according to its density. More houses and fewer open spaces suggest a higher density.

Alternatively, you can use the complexity of the road network to imply density: more streets means more tightly packed houses.

In case either of these sounds like too much work, there are other options:

- Cross-hatch each district according to its density level.
- Use graded colors such as yellow, orange and red.
- Repeat a district's outline for each level of density.

#### Roads and Transportation Networks

Start by drawing the main roads connecting important landmarks. Think about how people would travel most frequently, follow terrain and region shapes, and use variations of the grid to fill out your districts. Which are the arteries leading in and out of the city?

Try and distinguish between main and secondary roads, and feel free to omit smaller local roads. Remember that main avenues often feature unique names. Ensure that rail-lines, tram-lines, pedestrian roads, and highways can all be distinguished from each other.

#### Finishing Touches

A compass rose and/or a scale can help your map feel complete. A legend explaining what your symbols represent can be very handy too – if you have the space. If you don't, you can always put that information on the back.

## Rules Summary

### Phase Overview

#### Setup Phase

1. **Pick from the Scale–Age table**
   - Draw a number of boxes on an index card or piece of paper
2. **Pick from the Scale–Size table**
   - Draw a number of lines to split the map into regions
   - Set aside your citizen tokens
3. **Pick from the Scale–Influence table**
   - Set aside your power tokens

#### Founding Phase

1. **Roll on the Terrain–Geography table**
   - Draw the major terrain shape on the map
2. **Roll on the Terrain–Feature table** *(repeat 4 times)*
   - Draw the terrain feature on your map
   - Name the terrain feature
3. **Roll on the Purpose–Location table**
   - Draw a resource on the map
   - Name the resource
   - Move two power tokens from the growth pool to the resource
   - *(Add a landmark for the resource)*
4. **Roll on the Purpose–Decision table**
   - Draw the city's first district
   - Move a citizen token from the growth pool to the district
   - Name your district
5. **Roll on the Power–Paradigm table**
   - Note the name and symbol of this faction on an index card
   - Move 2 power tokens from the growth pool to the faction card
   - *(Add a landmark for that faction)*
6. **Roll on the Power–Relationship table**
   - Write the name and symbol of the faction on an index card
   - Move 1 power token from the growth pool to the faction card
   - *(Add a landmark for this faction)*
7. **Name the city** and write the name at the edge of your map
   - The player whose name got picked begins the next phase

#### Development Phase

1. **Roll on the Events table**
   - Perform the actions listed
   - *(Move up to two tokens from the growth pool to the city)* — optional
2. **Mark off a box on the timeline**
3. **Repeat** until the timeline is filled

#### Topping Out Phase

1. **Move two tokens from the growth pool to the city**
   - Repeat until all tokens are spent
2. **Add a second name for your city** and write it on the edge of your map
   - The player whose name got picked adds one last, unique landmark

### Action Overview

#### General Actions

**Adding a feature**
- Draw the feature on the map
- Name the feature

**Transforming a feature**
- *(Update the drawing)*
- *(Rename the feature)*

**Removing a feature**
- Update the drawing
- Move any tokens on it to the pool *(or elsewhere if you want)*
- *(Rename the feature)*

**Resources — Adding a Resource**
- Draw it on the map
- Name the resource
- Take 1+ power tokens from the pool *(if empty, take from factions/map)*
- *(Add a related landmark)*

#### Districts

**Adding a district**
- Draw it on the map
- Name the district
- Take 1 citizen token from the pool *(if empty, take 1 from a district)*

**Increasing district density**
- Take 1 citizen token from the pool *(if empty, take 1 from a district)*

**Reducing district density**
- Move 1+ citizen tokens to the pool
- If a district without tokens would lose density, remove it instead

**Removing a district**
- Update the drawing
- Distribute its citizen tokens among other districts and the growth pool
- Return all power tokens to the pool
- *(Rename the district)*

#### Factions

**Adding a faction**
- Draw its symbol on an index card
- Name the faction
- Take 1 power token from the map *(if none, take 1 from a faction; if no factions, take 1 from pool)*
- *(Add a related landmark)*

**Gaining power**
- Take 1+ power tokens from the map *(if none, take 1+ from a faction; if no factions, take 1+ from pool)*

**Losing power**
- Move 1+ power tokens to the pool
- If a faction without tokens loses power, remove it instead

**Removing a faction**
- Distribute its power tokens among factions, features, and the pool
- Remove the index card
- *(Remove or transform the associated landmark)*
- If this was the last faction, add a new faction with 1 power (from the removed faction, if possible)

## Tables

During many steps of the game you'll be asked to either pick an entry from a table or randomly determine one. To do the latter you will roll one or more dice, and then look for the corresponding number in the appropriate table to locate your entry.

### How to Use the Tables

#### 1. Determine your Entry

Sometimes you can freely pick an entry of your choice, but, if you have to randomly determine one, you need to roll some dice. There are three options:

- **D6:** Roll a single six-sided dice and use the number shown (1-6).
- **2D6:** Roll two six-sided dice and add the results together (2-12).
- **D666:** Roll three six-sided dice in sequence, with each dice representing a digit (111-666).

For the D666 roll you will need a method to put the dice in order before you roll. You can roll them in sequence, use colored dice and determine the order by color, or you can roll them and go from the leftmost to the rightmost.

> **Example:** A player wants to randomly find the geography of their city. They roll 2D6: a 3 and a 6, which adds up to a total of 9. They look at the Terrain–Geography table to find the entry with that number: **9 – Coastal**.

> **Example:** A player rolls 6, 1 and 4 in order for an event. This means they look at the entry for **614 – Drought**.

#### 2. Read Aloud

Once you've determined your result, read its name and description aloud for all players at the table.

Each table entry consists of a few things:
- **Number:** This shows you what number the entry corresponds to.
- **Name:** This is the title of the event.
- **Description:** This tells you what the event is about and may pose some interesting questions.
- **Actions:** Each entry may contain one or more actions that need to be taken.

#### 3. Find your Answers

Your job is to then make sense of the entry within the narrative of your city. Many entries will feature questions that provide a jumping off point for your narrative. They are mainly meant to inspire you – don't feel bound by them if they don't spark any ideas.

Let yourself be guided by the factions of your city and the events that have come before. This will help you to continue their arcs and build upon their stories.

#### 4. Perform Actions

After you've figured out what the story is, you may have to perform certain actions. There may be any number of actions, sometimes even requiring you to choose between multiple options or leaving it completely up to the player's choice.

- If an action cannot be taken, you cannot choose it. For example, if an event asks you to "Lose 1 power or remove a resource", and no faction has any power tokens, you are forced to remove a resource.
- Actions written in parentheses are optional. You can perform these if you feel that they fit the story you are telling. If not, don't worry about them.

Decide which of the given options best fits your urban narrative and then resolve the actions.
